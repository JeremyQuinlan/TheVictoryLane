Treasuries update: what's driving yields higher, and what could get them to go down

Sep 30, 2026 · 08:27 PM ET

This morning we have a strategy piece rather than a standard market note — a deep dive on what's been driving Treasury yields higher and what would need to happen to bring them back down. Let's walk through the key points because this has real implications for how you think about positioning right now. Yields have been pushed higher by a long list of forces — resilient economic growth, massive issuance from both governments and corporations tied to the data center boom, inflationary pressure from the wars in Ukraine and Iran, tariffs feeding into prices, a shrinking workforce due to immigration policy, and Fed credibility concerns around the transition from Powell to Warsh. That's a lot of headwinds stacked against the bond market, and most of them aren't going away anytime soon. On the inflation front, August PCE came in softer than expected, but the caveat is important — that was largely a statistical adjustment, not genuine disinflation. The August consumer and producer price readings were both quite hot, and September inflation is likely to look ugly because of what's happened with energy, specifically diesel prices, which bleed into core inflation in a very sticky way. So don't get too comfortable on the inflation front. Now, what could get yields lower? Growth slowdown would help, but too much of one would hurt stocks. Cooler inflation would help, but the near-term data pipeline looks rough. On the fiscal side, there is zero credible commitment to deficit reduction in Washington or Europe — the White House is still floating ideas like indexing capital gains for inflation and sending five thousand dollar checks to every American. Democrats are expected to take the House in November, which sets up fierce budget fights and potential shutdowns in the quarters ahead. For the Fed, the Warsh transition remains a source of unease — investors worry about politicization and how his task forces might reshape everything from inflation measurement to balance sheet management. His task force outcomes could start arriving as early as December, and a commitment to halt balance sheet shrinkage would be a meaningful positive for Treasuries. On the corporate side, watch Anthropic's S1 closely — it could hit soon. If the economics of frontier AI labs look more sustainable than feared, that could signal a slowdown in the heavy debt issuance that has been flooding the market. An Anthropic or OpenAI IPO would potentially serve the same function by reducing the industry's reliance on bond markets. Finally, stablecoins are a longer-term wildcard — if current growth trends hold, stablecoin issuers could be buying close to four hundred billion dollars of short-term Treasuries by the end of 2030, which is a non-trivial source of demand. Bottom line — yields are structurally elevated and the near-term path of least resistance remains higher. Stocks should be careful what they wish for when it comes to catalysts that could bring rates down, because most of them come with serious economic side effects.
